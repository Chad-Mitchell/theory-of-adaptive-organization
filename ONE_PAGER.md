# The cost of judgment

*Chad Mitchell. Public working draft, CC0. Not peer reviewed. The full argument, with every number, is [`CORE.md`](CORE.md); the front page is [`README.md`](README.md).*

Thermodynamics tells an engineer the best an engine can do, and how far a real one falls short. This theory aims to do the same for any system that must act before its world arrives: cells, colonies, brains, teams, firms, AI and robot systems. Its object is the cost of judgment, defined for any adaptive system: the heat a memory spends, plus the stake on being wrong, under a cap on the chance of ruin.

$$J = \dot Q + w\,\mathbb{E}[\ell] \qquad\text{subject to}\qquad P(\text{ruin})\le\varepsilon.$$

The law of adaptive organization (hypothesis): strongly selected systems minimize the cost of judgment.

If that holds, the gap to the minimum says where a system wastes and what would close it. The hypothesis is untested: no real system has been shown to sit at the minimum. The floor and the designs hold by proof, each inside named classes. Only the hypothesis can miss a measurement.

**What is proved, and where.** For bipartite Markov chains and additive-noise diffusions, in steady state the heat is at least \(kT\) times the rate at which the memory learns its world (Horowitz and Esposito 2014). Beyond those classes the floor is open. In the worked class of an exogenous equilibrium Ornstein–Uhlenbeck signal of rate \(a\) and variance \(v\), with mean-square loss and a memory that does not move its signal, the stake ratio is \(x=wv/(kT\,a)\). At or below \(x=1\) the cheapest design is blind and no memory pays. Above it the minimum cost is \(2\sqrt{kT\,a\,v\,w}-kT\,a\). The two-state telegraph, with Hamming loss and a memory that does not move its signal, is in that worked class and has a separate closed form.

These further results hold without a Gaussian or linear step: for systems meeting the representation axioms on at least three essential interval coordinates, a representing cost is a priced sum and the memory coordinate is Shannon information (the axioms do not choose the coordinates); memory costs heat in the classes above; on a fixed menu with the ruin cap slack, judgment spend does not fall as the stake rises; a boundary can be enough; convex payoffs gain from shocks, and caps can reverse that gain; zero-gain control drops out.

**The method.** Write the cost, find its minimum — or let a search scored by the cost find it — and read off the design. No single closed-form design exists for every system. In named classes, each in its own model, the minimum yields when memory pays, how many layers, what to share, where a boundary is enough, when a shock should strengthen a system rather than harm it, and when acting beats sensing alone. A team's minimized cost folds into its parent's problem, so the method nests, with a stated error when the team's cost does not enter as upkeep. Under weak-mutation selection with fitness linear in the cost, near a quadratic minimum in \(d\) coordinates the predicted excess is \(d/(2\beta)\), which shrinks as selection strength \(\beta\) rises. That route holds only under three conditions, each with a counterexample in our own runs: strong selection, a minimum the search can reach, and a cost that includes everything that matters.

**What would count against it, and what is next.** Before a measurement is a test, a class must qualify: a cost-to-fitness map, a population proxy, a band that excludes a named rival, and a redesign time with at least ten of those times in the same world. A qualifying design outside that band is a miss for that class, logged with its signed size and the update it drives (`process/PROCESS.md`). Without that check, a field result is not a test of the hypothesis. Every scored field row so far is NOT A TEST or INCONCLUSIVE. Next: the continuous-information extension of the memory axiom (T48), non-Gaussian closed forms (T49), then qualify one strongly selected system, preregister and hash-lock the prediction, and measure.
