So central < local ⟺ N²c > N(c+Nd) ⟺ d/c < 1 − 1/N (sympy: flat² − central² = 2Nq_g w(Nc − Nd − c)). With staleness N·wσ²·ℓ, ℓ_crit/T* = 1 − √((1+Nd/c)/N). Both are correct, under ρ = 1, α = 2, hold-last-value reads, unit loss weight, and local trackers co-located with their targets (zero local latency). These assumptions are stated, except the last, which is now stated.

**B2. The "no hubs / in-degree variance below Poisson" claim misreads Coutinho et al.** COSMIC_WEB.md:11, :21 and :80 report "in-degree variance 0.709 at ⟨k⟩ = 1 (Poisson would give 1)" as a property of the published cosmic-web networks. In the paper (p. 4), 0.709 is the **analytic value for M3 on a random galaxy distribution** ("networks constructed by the M3 model for a random galaxy distribution, the variance of the in-degree distribution is 0.709"). It does not describe the real web. The paper says the simulated and observed P(k_in) "deviate from the random distribution". Fig. 4(a), which I viewed at 300 dpi, shows the real-web in-degree at ⟨k⟩ = 20 *broader* than Poisson and broader than random points, out to the edge of the plot (k_in = 30). "Hubs are definitely absent" is the authors' inference, drawn next to the random-point number. The repo's own check agrees: at ⟨k⟩ = 1 Poisson points give 0.72, the random-point value. My check (D) gives in-degree var/mean 0.58 for Poisson points at k = 20, against 2.1 and 4.8 for clustered points. So clustering broadens the degree distribution, though not into a hub tail (max 48 at mean 20). The headline "the published web has no hubs" (also REACH.md:22, :152; THEORY.md:14; BRIEF.md:39; CLAIM_MAP N39 and N40) is therefore stronger than the source. *Fix applied:* the 0.709 is attributed correctly. The note now says the real web's in-degree is broader than Poisson and than random points, that no heavy tail is visible to k_in = 30, that no hub statistic is published, and that the M3 rule caps in-degree. The headline becomes "no hubs reported (no heavy tail seen; not tested with a hub statistic)". The point that degree heterogeneity beyond random positions is real gravitational structure is added.

## SHOULD-FIX (all applied)

**S1. ρ means something else than in T3a (WORMHOLES.md:33, :51).** T3a defines ρ = q_g/q_u, a ratio of shared to private drift rates (THEOREMS T3a; THEORY N2). WORMHOLES calls ρ a "shared fraction", uses ρ = 1 for "fully shared", and refers to "the 1−ρ part". Its "ρ = 1" is T3a's ρ → ∞. *Fix:* the definition is aligned with T3a. I also derived the partial-sharing generalization, which the self-review listed as missing. A hybrid (central for the shared driver, local reads for each residual) beats all-local at zero latency iff **d/c < 1/N\*(ρ) − 1/N**, with N\*(ρ) = (√(1+ρ)+1)²/ρ. At d = 0 this is exactly T3a; as ρ → ∞ it is d/c < 1 − 1/N. It is checked against brute-force minimization in 180/180 cases (check A). It is labelled DERIVED and, like T3a, sufficient (one feasible hybrid policy).

**S2. "Open versus finite is a secondary split" is too dismissive (WORMHOLES.md:10, :54; REACH.md:24; THEORY.md:529; BRIEF.md:40).** For the question LAT-1 answers (how far one tracker can reach), open versus finite is the decisive first split. My multi-hop simulation (check B) puts mouths at density 2, gives throats reach Λ and per-hop latency τ, and lets signals transfer at light speed. Latency then grows linearly with distance at slope ≥ τ/Λ: 0.138 for Λ = 1, τ = 0.1; 0.447 for τ = 0.3; 0.057 for Λ = 2, τ = 0.1. So a finite "tube map" is a faster medium with v_eff ≤ Λ/τ, and **LAT-1 survives with c replaced by v_eff**: federation is still forced beyond v_eff·(D*/q)^{1/(α−1)}. With open throats (any mouth to any mouth), or with τ = 0 on a percolating throat network, the slope is about 0 (−0.001, −0.003) and the distance limit disappears. Chad's split is the right first split for reach. The note's five quantities decide the second question, whether to centralize. *Fix:* the note says this plainly, adds §4b with the result, and keeps the five quantities for the centralization question.
