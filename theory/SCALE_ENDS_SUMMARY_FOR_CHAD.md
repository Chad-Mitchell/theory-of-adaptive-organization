# The ends of the scale, in plain English (18 Nov 2026; corrected after independent review)

**The costs are real at every scale.** What changes at the ends is whether a model is worth anything.

**Bottom.** Nothing threatens an atom, and it has no move to make, so a model would cost at least kT ln 2 per bit and buy nothing. Atoms do carry a little information about their surroundings (that's why they make good thermometers), but that's physics, not a model.

**Switch-on.** Models pay only when heredity is good enough (Eigen's limit), a choice depends on the world, the world drifts at a trackable pace, and the first bit is worth its cost. For a bacterium the energy floor is far from binding.

**Top.** Stars burn fuel but track nothing outside: held, no model. A black hole is the extreme of the frozen class: the most information possible for its size, at the lowest energy per bit for that size, no upkeep. It leaks away over ~10⁶⁷ years × (solar masses)³. Three numbers are readable; the rest returns scrambled only after about the Page time (~0.54 of its lifetime, so ~10⁶⁷ years for a Sun-mass hole, ∝ M³), so nothing uses it as memory. It records the past and predicts nothing.

**How high.** To the cosmic horizon: memory cap ~3×10¹²² nats, a floor price per erased bit (Landauer). Light delay forces big structures to federate (testable on Earth).

**Labels.** Derived: both ends, the black hole's place (given known physics; lateness counted as error), the light-delay bound. Hypothesis: the switch-on conditions. Credited: Prigogine, Pross, Kolchinsky–Wolpert, Smolin, Simon. Rejected: atoms predict; black holes think; the theory explains why black holes exist.

**Opening:** "An atom never has to guess. Physics holds it together for free, and nothing it could do would change its fate. So a model would cost it something and buy it nothing. Knowing starts to pay only when a changing world can ruin you, and you have a move to make."

**Closing:** "At the far end is the black hole: the most memory physics allows in a space that size, and almost none of it readable. It keeps a scrambled record of what fell in, and predicts nothing. Everything that has to keep up lives in between."
