Judgment: staying alive under the second law
A Theory of Adaptive Organization, November 11, 2026

One principle runs from atoms to cities, the second law: available free energy gets dissipated. Atoms and molecules follow it on average, settling and reacting with nothing to maintain. Cells, people, firms and cities stay far from equilibrium by taking in free energy and dissipating it. To keep going they can't wait for the world to arrive. Once acting late is costly, it can pay to carry a model and act ahead. That's judgment: not a new law, but a way of staying alive under the old one.

The physics: information has a thermodynamic floor. Erasing one bit releases on average at least kT ln 2 of heat (Landauer; confirmed in experiment). Still, Sivak, Bell and Crooks (2012) showed that, for a system driven by a fluctuating signal it doesn't act back on, memory that doesn't predict the signal sets a floor on dissipated work. Most real systems pay far above these floors. In information terms this memory-versus-prediction tradeoff has the form of the information bottleneck (Tishby, Pereira and Bialek, 1999): keep as few bits as possible for a chosen level of prediction. Still et al. give a thermodynamic counterpart.

The bet: every self-maintaining system, from a cell to a firm, pays to keep a model, even an implicit one, and faces the same tradeoff between what it remembers, what it predicts and what it spends. The erasure floor applies everywhere, since every bit is erased in a cell, a brain or a disk. For a cell, the energy spent sensing and remembering is a real constraint (Lan et al., 2012). For a firm the floor is trillions of times below what memory actually costs in salaries and attention: true, but it constrains nothing. The bet is that the same tradeoff still shapes efficient design there, paid in time and money instead of heat.

What follows from those costs, not from the physics alone. An efficient self-maintaining model should:
1. Return what matters early enough to act before the window closes. Interrupt unasked only when stake × chance it's relevant, plus what the outcome would teach, beats the cost of interrupting. A late prediction buys nothing, and an uncertain unasked guess that is never tested produces no outcome to learn from.
2. Keep a fact while chance it's needed × cost of missing it beats the cost of keeping it current, which rises with how fast it drifts. When that flips, let it fade, recoverably. Memory that predicts nothing needed is cost with no return.
3. Move a belief further the more reliable the new signal is relative to the belief. A trusted correction replaces it; a secondhand or inferred one moves it partway.
4. Keep a prior version recoverable until its correction proves out; after that it can go. A wrong overwrite costs more than the version it saved.
5. Act from the current model, not a copy of it. Recheck the source when a copy's likely error, from hops plus drift × age, is too big for the stakes.
6. Record whether each prediction was used, ignored or late; without outcomes nothing learns what to return. Use itself can carry the signal, with no one in charge: links used together strengthen (Hebb, 1949), and in later work unused ones fade. Ant trails too: used ones are reinforced, unused ones evaporate. Hypothesis: the same mechanism at every level.
7. Spend more effort watching as drift × stakes rises, most where shocks are large relative to buffers. Slow, low-stakes facts get almost none.

How it scales: parts linked tightly inside a unit and loosely across units give fast dynamics inside and slow dynamics between (Simon, 1962). Kiebel et al. (2008) proposed that in the brain, higher levels track slower causes.

Temperature beyond molecules: Drăgulescu and Yakovenko (2000) showed that in models where money is conserved in random exchanges, it spreads like energy in a gas, with a "temperature" equal to average money per person. Mine differs: not thermal jostling against the barriers holding things in place, but the typical shock a firm's or city's parts face relative to the buffer each holds. The form is the same, so some math carries over: if shocks are independent, their accumulated spread grows with the square root of time. But it's measured in dollars or orders, not kelvin, and no physical law sets it.

Predictions, each wrong if a fair test fails it, with measures fixed in advance and tested on data not used to form them:
- Volatility killing firms is known. The bet is the interaction: where shocks are large relative to buffers, slow error-fixers fall apart faster than fast ones, with risk tracking shock size × √(time to fix) ÷ buffer, the form of Merton's distance to default with time to fix as the horizon. At low shock levels speed barely matters. First test: open-source projects.
- Effort spent watching a fact rises with how fast it drifts and with what staleness costs.
- A copied fact's error grows with its hops plus its drift × time since the source was last checked.
- Stress strengthens a system up to a ceiling, and faster error correction raises the ceiling.

What would kill it: fair tests at two or more scales where these predictions fail. If they hold at firm scale, that's evidence the math generalizes, not that thermodynamics is doing the work there.

Hypotheses, not findings. I'll test what I can and post results either way. Try to break it.
